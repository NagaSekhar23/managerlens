# Difficult Conversations

> General management guidance for ManagerLens. This is not official company policy and does not
> reference any specific organization, employee, or legal framework. Adapt it to your own context.

## Prepare the opening line deliberately

The first sentence of a difficult conversation sets its entire tone. An opening that states a
neutral, specific observation ("I wanted to talk about what happened in yesterday's meeting")
lands very differently from one that opens with judgment ("I need to talk to you about your
attitude"). Rehearsing the first sentence, specifically, is one of the highest-leverage things a
manager can do before a hard conversation.

## Separate intent from impact

People often explain their own behavior by intent ("I was just trying to help") while judging
others' behavior by impact ("that felt dismissive"). Naming this gap explicitly — "I don't know
what your intent was here, but I want to tell you the impact it had" — keeps the conversation
from becoming a debate about what someone "really meant," which is usually unresolvable and
beside the point.

## Expect and allow silence

Difficult conversations often include pauses that feel unbearably long to the person leading
them. Resist the urge to fill silence with more talking, justification, or softening. Giving the
other person space to actually process what was said, rather than immediately following it with
reassurance, often produces a more honest and useful response.

## Don't negotiate away the core message

It's common to soften a hard message so much in the moment that the actual point gets lost. If
the purpose of the conversation is to convey that something needs to change, that message needs
to survive the conversation intact, even while being delivered with empathy. A useful check
afterward: could the other person accurately repeat back the main point in one sentence?

## Plan for more than one reaction

People respond to hard news differently — defensiveness, tears, silence, immediate problem-solving,
or delayed reaction after they've had time to process. Having even a rough sense of how the
specific person tends to react, and giving yourself permission to pause or reschedule if the
conversation isn't going anywhere productive, prevents a single bad exchange from becoming
irreversible.

## Close with a concrete next step

Ending a difficult conversation without a clear next step (a follow-up meeting, an action item,
a timeline) leaves both people unsure whether anything was actually resolved. Even a small,
concrete next step turns a hard conversation into progress rather than just an unpleasant event.
