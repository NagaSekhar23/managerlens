# Conflict Resolution

> General management guidance for ManagerLens. This is not official company policy and does not
> reference any specific organization, employee, or legal framework. Adapt it to your own context.

## Distinguish task conflict from relationship conflict

Disagreement about how to do the work (task conflict) is often healthy and can improve
decisions. Disagreement rooted in personal friction, distrust, or perceived disrespect
(relationship conflict) tends to be corrosive and rarely resolves on its own. The same surface
behavior — two people arguing in a meeting — can be either, and misdiagnosing which one you're
dealing with leads to the wrong intervention. Task conflict often benefits from a clearer
decision-making process; relationship conflict needs a direct, private conversation about the
relationship itself.

## Talk to each person separately first

Before bringing two people together to resolve a conflict, it's usually worth hearing each
person's account separately. This surfaces information that might not come out in a joint
conversation (out of politeness or escalation dynamics), and lets you understand whether the
conflict is symmetric or whether one person feels significantly more affected than the other.

## Avoid playing judge

When a manager positions themselves as deciding who was "right," the conversation often shifts
into each party building a case, rather than looking for a way forward. It's usually more
productive to focus less on litigating the past and more on defining what needs to be true going
forward for both people to work together effectively — while still naming any behavior that was
genuinely out of line.

## Watch for withdrawal, not just visible conflict

Some of the most damaging conflict is silent: people quietly avoiding each other, routing around
each other on projects, or disengaging rather than raising the issue directly. If you notice two
people who used to collaborate no longer doing so, it's worth checking in on both, even in the
absence of any visible incident.

## Bring in a neutral structure for recurring conflict

If the same two people keep having the same disagreement, the issue is often structural, not
personal — unclear ownership, competing incentives, or overlapping responsibilities. In these
cases, resolving the immediate disagreement without addressing the underlying structure usually
means the same conflict recurs within weeks.

## Know when to involve HR

Conflict that includes any element of harassment, discrimination, retaliation, or threats is
outside the scope of informal conflict resolution and should be routed to HR or the appropriate
internal function. This guidance covers ordinary interpersonal friction, not conduct issues.
