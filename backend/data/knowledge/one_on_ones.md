# One-on-Ones

> General management guidance for ManagerLens. This is not official company policy and does not
> reference any specific organization, employee, or legal framework. Adapt it to your own context.

## The agenda belongs to the employee, mostly

A one-on-one that's entirely status updates duplicates what could be an email or a team
stand-up, and trains the employee to bring nothing but task updates. The most useful one-on-ones
give the employee the majority of the agenda: what's on their mind, what's blocking them, what
they want feedback on, and how they're doing more broadly. The manager's job is largely to ask
good questions and listen.

## Consistency matters more than length

A 20-minute one-on-one that happens reliably every week builds more trust than an hour-long
one that gets rescheduled repeatedly. Frequent cancellation of one-on-ones sends a strong,
usually unintended signal that the relationship is low priority. If a one-on-one must be
shortened or moved, it's worth acknowledging that explicitly rather than letting it quietly slip.

## Use them to catch small things early

Because one-on-ones are private and recurring, they're often where early signals of disengagement,
confusion, or friction with a teammate surface — well before they'd show up in team-wide metrics
or a formal review. Treating a one-on-one purely as project status tracking misses this function
entirely. Simple, open questions ("how are you feeling about the team right now?") create room
for things that wouldn't otherwise come up.

## Don't only talk about work

Some managers avoid any personal topics to stay "professional," which can make one-on-ones feel
transactional and can also mean missing context that matters (a change in circumstances that's
affecting someone's capacity or mood). This doesn't mean prying — it means leaving room, and
following the employee's lead on how much they want to share.

## Keep a shared, running document

A shared notes document that carries over from one one-on-one to the next — with open items,
things to follow up on, and prior commitments — prevents both people from having to remember
everything, and creates a lightweight record of what's been discussed and agreed over time.

## Notice patterns across time, not just single sessions

A single quiet one-on-one might mean nothing. Three quiet one-on-ones in a row, or a pattern of
an employee giving shorter and shorter answers, is a signal worth naming directly rather than
letting it continue unaddressed: "I've noticed our last few check-ins have felt different than
usual — is everything okay?"
