# Giving Feedback

> General management guidance for ManagerLens. This is not official company policy and does not
> reference any specific organization, employee, or legal framework. Adapt it to your own context.

## Separate observation from interpretation

Effective feedback describes specific, observable behavior rather than character traits or
assumed motives. "You missed the last two sprint deadlines" is observable. "You're not
committed to this team" is an interpretation, and stating it as fact tends to make people
defensive rather than reflective. When giving feedback, lead with what was actually seen or
heard, and hold your interpretation as a hypothesis to check, not a conclusion to deliver.

## Timeliness matters more than politeness

Feedback loses value the longer it's delayed. A manager who saves up minor concerns for a
quarterly review often finds the employee is blindsided, because small issues compounded
silently for months. Where reasonable, address specific incidents close to when they happen,
briefly and privately, rather than batching them into a single high-stakes conversation later.

## Make feedback actionable

Vague feedback ("communicate better") gives the recipient nothing to actually change. Effective
feedback names the specific behavior, its concrete impact, and what a better version would look
like: "In yesterday's client call, you interrupted the client twice while they were describing
the issue. That made it harder for them to finish explaining, and we had to re-ask a question
we'd already covered. Next time, let's try letting them finish before responding."

## Balance is not about a ratio, it's about accuracy

Some frameworks suggest fixed ratios of positive to critical feedback. In practice, the more
useful principle is accuracy: give positive feedback when something genuinely went well, and
critical feedback when something genuinely needs to change. Manufacturing praise to soften
criticism, or delivering criticism sandwiched between compliments as a formula, tends to erode
trust once people notice the pattern.

## Check for understanding, not just delivery

Delivering feedback is not the same as it landing. After sharing feedback, it helps to ask the
person to reflect it back in their own words, or to ask an open question like "how does that
match what you were seeing?" This surfaces misunderstandings immediately rather than after the
person has already acted on a misreading of what you meant.

## Written feedback carries risk

Tone is easy to misread in text. Feedback that involves any emotional weight, ambiguity, or
potential for the recipient to feel criticized is usually better delivered in a conversation
(in person or by video/call) where you can read reactions and adjust in real time, with a brief
written summary afterward if a record is useful.
