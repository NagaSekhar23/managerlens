# Performance Conversations

> General management guidance for ManagerLens. This is not official company policy and does not
> reference any specific organization, employee, or legal framework. Adapt it to your own context.

## Start from evidence, not conclusions

A performance conversation goes off the rails quickly if it opens with a verdict ("your
performance has slipped") rather than the evidence behind it. Start by naming the specific,
observable pattern — missed deadlines, quality issues, absences from key meetings — and let the
employee respond before drawing conclusions together. This also protects against acting on an
incomplete picture: there may be a cause (workload, unclear expectations, a personal situation)
that changes what "good" support looks like.

## Distinguish a skill gap from a will gap

Underperformance usually falls into one of two categories: the person doesn't yet know how to do
the thing (skill), or they know how but something is getting in the way of doing it consistently
(will, or circumstance). These require different responses. A skill gap benefits from training,
pairing, or clearer documentation. A will/circumstance gap benefits from understanding the
blocker directly — workload, unclear priorities, conflict with a teammate, something outside
work. Treating a skill gap as a motivation problem (or vice versa) usually makes things worse.

## Set a concrete definition of "better"

Performance conversations often fail because "improvement" was never defined in a way both
people would recognize when they saw it. Wherever possible, agree on specific, observable
markers: what would need to be true in four weeks for both of you to agree things are back on
track? Vague aspirations ("be more proactive") should be translated into specific behaviors.

## Document lightly, but do document

Even outside a formal process, it helps to send a short written summary after a substantive
performance conversation: what was discussed, what was agreed, and by when you'll check back in.
This isn't about building a case against someone — it protects both parties from a fuzzy memory
of what was actually agreed, and gives the employee something concrete to work from.

## Recognize when the conversation is bigger than one manager

Patterns involving potential harassment, discrimination, safety, or legal exposure are outside
the scope of an informal management conversation and should involve HR or the appropriate
internal function before you proceed. This guidance is about everyday performance coaching, not
about handling situations with legal or compliance dimensions.

## Follow-through is the actual intervention

The conversation itself rarely changes behavior on its own — the follow-through does. A single
well-run conversation without a scheduled check-in tends to fade into "that thing we talked about
once." Put a concrete follow-up on the calendar before the conversation ends, even if it's brief.
