# Psychological Safety

> General management guidance for ManagerLens. This is not official company policy and does not
> reference any specific organization, employee, or legal framework. Adapt it to your own context.

## Psychological safety is not the same as comfort

Psychological safety means people believe they can raise a concern, admit a mistake, or ask a
"dumb" question without being humiliated or punished for it — it does not mean the absence of
disagreement, challenge, or accountability. Teams with high psychological safety often have
more direct disagreement, not less, because people trust that surfacing a problem won't be held
against them.

## How a manager reacts to bad news sets the norm

The single biggest lever a manager has over psychological safety is how they respond the first
few times someone brings them a mistake or a piece of bad news. A defensive, blaming, or
dismissive reaction — even once — teaches everyone watching that raising problems is risky, and
that lesson is very slow to unlearn. A calm, curious response ("thanks for telling me, walk me
through what happened") builds the opposite norm quickly.

## Model fallibility yourself

Teams take cues from what a manager does, not just what they say. A manager who never admits
uncertainty, never says "I don't know," and never acknowledges their own mistakes implicitly
signals that vulnerability is unsafe — even if they explicitly say otherwise. Visibly modeling
"I got that wrong" or "I'm not sure, let's figure it out together" does more than any stated
value.

## Silence in a meeting is not agreement

In a team without psychological safety, silence is often a sign that people don't feel safe
raising an objection, not that everyone agrees. If a meeting reliably produces universal
agreement with no pushback, that's worth treating as a signal to investigate, not a sign of
alignment. Explicitly inviting dissent ("what's the strongest argument against this?") can
surface things a general "any objections?" won't.

## Safety can differ across a team

Psychological safety isn't uniform even within one team — newer employees, people from
underrepresented groups, or people who've had a bad experience previously often report feeling
less safe than others on the same team, even under the same manager. It's worth checking in on
this directly and individually rather than assuming a team-wide impression applies to everyone.

## Accountability and safety reinforce each other

Psychological safety without clear standards can drift into low performance going unaddressed.
The most effective teams pair high safety (comfortable raising problems) with high standards
(clear expectations and follow-through) — neither alone is sufficient.
