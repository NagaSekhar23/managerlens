# Goal Setting

> General management guidance for ManagerLens. This is not official company policy and does not
> reference any specific organization, employee, or legal framework. Adapt it to your own context.

## Vague goals produce vague accountability

A goal like "improve code quality" or "be more strategic" can't really be missed, because it was
never defined precisely enough to be checked. Effective goals specify what would count as done,
by when, and ideally how it will be measured or observed. If two reasonable people could disagree
about whether a goal was met, the goal needs to be more specific.

## Fewer goals, held more seriously

A long list of goals usually means none of them get real attention, and it becomes easy for
underperformance on any single one to be excused by busyness elsewhere. Two or three goals that
are genuinely tracked and discussed regularly tend to produce more actual progress than eight
goals that are set once and revisited only at review time.

## Separate outcome goals from the plan to get there

An outcome goal ("reduce customer-reported bugs by half this quarter") and the plan to achieve it
(more test coverage, a new review process) are different things. Conflating them means that if the
plan turns out to be wrong, the goal itself gets abandoned rather than the plan being adjusted. It
helps to revisit the plan frequently while holding the outcome goal more stably.

## Involve the person in setting their own goals

Goals handed down without any input from the person doing the work tend to produce compliance at
best. Goals that the employee had a real hand in shaping — even if the manager ultimately guides
the direction — tend to produce more ownership and more accurate goals, since the person doing
the work often has better information about what's actually achievable.

## Revisit goals when context changes

A goal set at the start of a quarter can become irrelevant or actively counterproductive if
priorities shift, a project is cancelled, or new information emerges. Treating goals as fixed
regardless of context can lead to people optimizing for a now-meaningless target instead of what
actually matters. A brief goal check-in partway through the period, not just at the end, catches
this early.

## Distinguish stretch goals from baseline expectations

If a goal is genuinely a stretch (something the person might reasonably not fully achieve even
while doing good work), that should be explicit. Treating a stretch goal as a baseline
expectation — and then treating a good-faith miss as underperformance — punishes ambition and
teaches people to set safer, smaller goals in the future.
